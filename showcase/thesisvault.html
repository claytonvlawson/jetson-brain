you>What is language deprivation and why does it matter for deaf children?
retrieved 4 passages + 2 linked notesmodel qwen2.5:3banswered on-device in 9.8s

Language deprivation refers to the lack of access to a natural language, particularly sign language, during early childhood development in deaf individuals. This can result from various factors such as being born to hearing parents who do not use sign language, or from policies that restrict the use of sign language.

Why it matters for deaf children:

  • Negative outcomes: Depriving deaf children of a natural language like sign language can lead to poor age-expected language, cognitive, and academic outcomes. Deaf children whose first exposure to sign language is not fluent, or occurs later in life, tend to have worse developmental trajectories than those exposed early.
  • Control case: Native signers (deaf children of Deaf parents) show age-expected language, cognitive, and academic outcomes similar to hearing peers, which indicates that natural sign language exposure can mitigate the effects of deprivation.
  • Clinical and research importance: Establishing language deprivation as a clinical-developmental construct matters for public-health and medical venues. Work like Murray, Hall & Snoddon (2019), Necessity of Signed Languages, published in the Bulletin of the WHO, demonstrates the weight of this argument.
  • Prevention: Sustained early sign exposure can close much of the outcome gap between deaf children with and without early language access.

In summary, ensuring that deaf children have access to a natural language during early development is critical for their linguistic, cognitive, and academic success.

you>How does Wyatte Hall frame the public-health stakes of early language access?
retrieved 4 passages + 2 linked notesmodel qwen2.5:3banswered on-device in 10.3s

Wyatte C. Hall frames the public-health stakes of early language access in several key ways:

  • Preventable harm: Denying deaf infants exposure to sign language is a preventable developmental harm with lifelong consequences, not merely a cultural debate (Hall 2017).
  • Neurodevelopmental disorder: He formalizes Language Deprivation Syndrome as a neurodevelopmental disorder with sociocultural origins, with irreversible neurological changes when language is introduced late (Hall, Levin & Anderson 2017).
  • Mental-health outcomes: Early deprivation is linked to increased depression, anxiety, and trauma exposure in adulthood (Murray, Hall & Snoddon 2024; Hall, Dye & Siddiqi 2023).
  • A global crisis: Fewer than 2% of deaf children worldwide receive early sign language access, making it a significant public-health issue (Glickman & Hall 2018).
  • Intersectionality: Race, rurality, and socioeconomic status intersect with the incidence of language deprivation, deepening health disparities (Hall et al. 2018).
  • Policy and practice: He places responsibility on clinical gatekeepers such as audiologists and early-intervention professionals, and calls for training to recognize linguistic injury (Hall, Levin & Anderson 2017; Caselli, Hall & Henner 2020).

Together these points frame early sign language access as a public-health measure to prevent lifelong adverse outcomes.

Why build it this way

Private by construction, not by promise

🔒

Nothing leaves the device

The model, the index, and the notes all live on the Jetson. There is no account, no telemetry, and no third party in the loop. Sensitive research reading stays local.

📚

Grounded in real scholarship

Every answer is built from and cited to the peer-reviewed sources in the vault, so a claim can always be traced back to Humphries, Hall, Padden, the WFD, and the rest.

🌱

Free and low-power

One inexpensive edge computer, no monthly bill, and a power draw closer to a lightbulb than a server. The whole system is a proof that useful, honest AI does not require the cloud.
